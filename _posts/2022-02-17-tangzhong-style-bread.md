*Adapted from P.J. Hamel's process on the King Arthur Baking blog.*

This is an Asian bread-baking technique that results in softer bread with a longer shelf life. By cooking a slurry using 10% of the flour and about 50% of the liquid in a recipe, you allow the starches in the flour to absorb more water (flour absorbs about twice as much hot liquid as cool/lukewarm). The result is a bread that is easier to knead, rises higher, is moister and softer due to more liquid retention, and goes stale more slowly due to the increased water content.

## Converting a recipe to use this method

1. Choose an appropriate bread recipe: a yeast bread intended to be soft, tender, and light (i.e. not a baguette or bagel)
2. Determine the recipe's hydration
    - Divide the weight of the liquid by the weight of the flour, then multiply by 100
    - This is a baker's method of calculating hydration: a *ratio* of *liquid to flour*, not the *percentage* of liquid in the total recipe
    - If the recipe contains large amounts of non-water ingredients that contain water (e.g. eggs, honey, butter, milk), use the chart following these instructions to calculate hydration
3. Scale the water weight up to reach a hydration of 75%:
    1. Multiply the weight of the flour by 0.75
    2. Add liquid to the recipe until the liquid weighs the amount calculated in step 1
4. Cook about 10% of the flour and half the liquid into a slurry
5. Let the slurry cool and mix in remaining ingredients, and proceed with recipe as directed

## Common ingredients' hydration levels

If your recipe uses a large volume of these ingredients in addition to or instead of water, use the following table to calculate hydration.

| Ingredient | Hydration (%) |
|---|---|
| Milk | 87 |
| Large egg | 74 |
| Honey | 17 |
| Butter (American) | 16 |
| Oil | 0 |

## Notes

- To convert a recipe for a no-knead bread that uses a long rise, reduce the amount of yeast to avoid generating too much acidity during the fermentation
- More is not better in this case; don't use more than 10% of the flour in the slurry since that will interfere too much with gluten development
